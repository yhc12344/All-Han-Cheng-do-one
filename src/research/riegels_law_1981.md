# Athletic Records and Human Endurance

**Author:** Peter S. Riegel  
**Journal:** American Scientist (Vol. 69, No. 3, pp. 285-290)  
**Year:** 1981  

---

## 🔬 Core Theory
Human endurance performances across distances between 3.5 minutes and 230 minutes follow a precise mathematical power law. As the distance increases, the sustainable speed decays relative to a standard fatigue factor:

$$T_2 = T_1 \cdot \left( \frac{D_2}{D_1} \right)^{b}$$

Where:
*   $T_1, T_2$: Recorded and predicted completion times.
*   $D_1, D_2$: Recorded and target distances.
*   $b$: The fatigue decay exponent.

## 📈 The Exponent Constant ($b = 1.06$)
Riegel's research across multiple cohorts established $1.06$ as the standard baseline exponent for running.
*   **$b = 1.06$:** Standard, well-trained recreational runner.
*   **$b < 1.06$ (e.g. 1.04 - 1.05):** Exceptionally high aerobic base or slow-twitch dominant athlete (e.g., diesel engine). Decays less over long distances.
*   **$b > 1.06$ (e.g. 1.07 - 1.10):** Fast-twitch dominant or under-trained runner. Experience severe pace drops over longer races (e.g., Marathon).

## 🏃 Split Pacing Strategies
Riegel's endurance decay dictates how splits should be managed:
*   **Negative Splits:** Conserves glycogen in the first half ($3\%$ slower than average), then uses mechanical efficiency to accelerate in the second half ($3\%$ faster). This mimics the body's natural cardiovascular warm-up drift.
*   **Positive Splits:** Exceeding target pace early ($2\%$ faster) leads to early glycogen depletion, causing a severe positive split ($3\%$ slower) in the final quarters due to neuromuscular fatigue.
